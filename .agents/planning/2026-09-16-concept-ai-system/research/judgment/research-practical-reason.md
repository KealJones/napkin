## 1. Dancy's moral particularism and the holism of reasons

**The holism thesis, precisely stated.** Dancy's holism of reasons is a modal
claim about the behavior of reasons across contexts: a consideration that
counts in favor of an action in one situation *can* — not must — count against
it, or count for nothing at all, in another situation. His own example: "the
fact that a remark would be funny might be, in one case a reason for making
the remark, in another case a reason against making the remark, and in still
another case no reason at all." Holism is explicitly compatible with some
reasons being invariant; the claim is only that reason-hood is not guaranteed
to travel. The opposing view, which Dancy calls **atomism** (attributed to
W.D. Ross), holds that if a consideration is a reason in one context it is a
reason with the same polarity wherever it recurs — context can change its
*weight* but not its *direction*.

**Holism vs. mere defeasibility — the crux.** This is the distinction the
whole debate turns on, and it is easy to flatten by accident. "Outweighed" and
"defeated" are atomist-friendly notions: the reason is still there, still
pointing the same direction, it has just lost a competition against a
stronger consideration on the other side. Holism claims something stronger
and structurally different: the *same feature*, in a different context, can
flip from a reason-for to a reason-against, or vanish as a reason altogether
— not because it got outgunned, but because the context changed what that
feature *is doing* normatively. A promise made under duress is not "a normal
promise-reason that lost to a bigger reason"; on Dancy's picture it may not
generate a reason to keep it at all. Contextualists who blur this — treating
holism as just "reasons are weighted differently in different contexts" — are
(per the SEP entry) missing why holism is a genuinely different, more
disruptive thesis than defeasibility: defeasibility leaves the atomist
inventory of reasons intact and only reshuffles outcomes; holism denies that
a fixed inventory of reasons-with-fixed-valence exists to be reshuffled.

**Enablers/disablers, intensifiers/attenuators.** Because holism needs
something to do the polarity-flipping work without those somethings
themselves becoming "part of the reason" (which would collapse holism back
into atomism about a bigger, disjunctive reason), Dancy introduces
favor-independent contextual roles: **defeaters** kill a reason's force
entirely (an offensive joke's cruelty defeats its being funny as a reason to
tell it); **enablers** switch a latent reason on (a receptive audience enables
funniness to count as a reason at all); **intensifiers** and **attenuators**
turn the volume up or down on a reason that is already active. These are
deliberately not themselves reasons — they are conditions on reasons — which
is the load-bearing move that keeps holism from being just "everything is
secretly one big context-relative reason."

**The argument against principles.** Dancy does not claim holism strictly
entails particularism (no exceptionless moral principles). His stronger move
is rhetorical/explanatory: if reasons behave holistically, any principle that
happens to hold without exception would be a "cosmic accident" — a
regularity with no metaphysical ground, since nothing forces the
context-sensitivity to bottom out in a finite, statable, exceptionless rule.
"Why should the heart of a discipline be the search for cosmic accidents?"
Ethics without Principles (2004) generalizes the earlier Moral Reasons (1993)
argument into a full anti-codifiability thesis: moral thought is possible,
and moral knowledge can be perfectly good knowledge, without any principles
at its foundation.

**The critics, fairly represented — this is a live fight, not a settled
one.**
- **Brad Hooker** ("Moral Particularism: Wrong and Bad," in Hooker & Little
  2000) argues principles need not be cosmic accidents: if rightness or
  goodness is grounded in a genuine underlying property (e.g., a hedonic or
  welfarist account of the good), the corresponding principle reflects that
  metaphysical grounding rather than floating free as an accident. He also
  challenges Dancy's dismissal of highly specific principles by giving cases
  where a narrow, situation-matched principle does real action-guiding work.
- **Margaret Little** (with Mark Lance, and independently in "Moral
  Generalities Revisited," in Hooker & Little 2000) grants the uncodifiability
  point for *fully specified, exceptionless, contentful* generalizations but
  defends **defeasible generalizations** — "lying is wrong" as true under
  normal/default conditions, with holistic exceptions understood as cases
  where the point or rationale of the generalization itself dissolves (the
  murderer-at-the-door case isn't a counterexample to "lying is wrong," it's
  a case where the generalization's rationale doesn't apply). This is a
  genuine middle position, not a capitulation to atomism.
- **Frank Jackson, Philip Pettit, and Michael Smith** ("Ethical Particularism
  and Patterns," in Hooker & Little 2000) press a cognitive-plausibility
  objection: particularism seems to require that a competent moral judge has
  a *skill* not grounded in any ability to recognize and represent patterns
  in the world — which they find psychologically implausible as an account
  of how judgment actually works. Skilled discrimination, on their view,
  has to bottom out in pattern recognition, and patterns are exactly the kind
  of thing principles state.
- **Sean McKeever and Michael Ridge**, most fully in *Principled Ethics:
  Generalism as a Regulative Ideal* (2006), argue directly that holism does
  not support particularism at all: on either available reading of holism,
  the phenomenon is compatible with — indeed is exactly what you'd expect
  from — a sufficiently sophisticated system of principles (principles with
  built-in conditions covering the enabling/disabling factors). Their
  positive view absorbs holism as a fact about reasons while insisting
  generalism remains the right regulative ideal for ethical theorizing.

**State of the debate.** The relevant SEP entry (Moral Particularism and
Moral Generalism) treats this as an open, active research program, not a
resolved question — intermediate positions keep appearing (Holton's
"principled particularism," with principles carrying implicit "that's it"
clauses; Stangl's "asymmetrical virtue particularism"), and the debate has
spread into grounding metaphysics, philosophy of language (generics), and
moral psychology. There is no consensus that particularism is defeated, nor
that it has won; both sides regard the exchange as substantive rather than
merely verbal.

*Bearing:* Dancy's holism is the most structurally exact match for the
Better(Gain, Loss) failure — a feature (weight loss, gaining money) whose
normative valence isn't fixed but flips depending on context in a way that
resists being captured as "outweighed." The Little/McKeever-Ridge side of the
debate is the strongest case that this needn't be fatal to codification —
it's fatal only to codification without conditions.

## 2. Aristotle: phronesis and the doctrine of the mean

Aristotle's practical wisdom (*phronesis*) is explicitly not reducible to
rule-following, and he gives a principled reason why, not just an empirical
observation that people don't follow rules. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* he
insists that ethical inquiry should not seek more precision than its subject
matter admits: "in this field many generalizations hold only for the most
part," and a well-educated person expects only the degree of exactness the
subject allows (1094b11–22). This is a methodological claim about the
*kind* of knowledge ethics is — not deficient science, but a different
category of knowing (practical, not theoretical/scientific *episteme*).
Circumstances vary too much from occasion to occasion for a finite rule set
to "collectively solve every practical problem."

The **doctrine of the mean** is often flattened into "virtue = the arithmetic
average of two vices," which Aristotle explicitly rejects. He contrasts a
fixed arithmetic mean (6 is always the midpoint of 10 and 2) with the mean
"relative to us," which a virtuous person must locate anew for the particular
circumstances (1106a36–b7). It is a state or disposition (*hexis*) — a
region a character occupies — not a single point derived from a formula, and
identifying it in a given situation is itself an exercise of judgment, not a
lookup. This "range, not maximum" framing is worth taking seriously on its
own terms: it says the good state is not "the most X" or "the optimal
trade-off value" but a way of being disposed that can be realized correctly
across a range of particular responses, with what counts as hitting the mean
differing case by case (sometimes intense anger is the mean; sometimes mild
anger is the mean; sometimes no anger).

The person of practical wisdom "sees the truth in each case, being as it
were a standard and measure" (1113a32–3) — a *perceptual* metaphor Aristotle
uses deliberately. The relevant perception is of *particulars*: phronesis
involves grasping the ethically salient features of a concrete situation
directly, not inferring them from a general premise via a syllogism whose
major premise is a moral rule. Aristotle is explicit that virtue of
character and phronesis are mutually constitutive — you cannot have full
practical wisdom without settled good character, and you cannot have virtue
of character (properly, rather than by luck) without phronesis to specify
what it requires here.

*Bearing:* the mean-as-range is a genuinely different shape than "compute a
resolution and pick a winner" — it suggests correctness is a region
compatible with several different concrete resolutions, not a single scalar
optimum, which is a different failure mode than the ones the formal-methods
pass surfaced.

## 3. Anscombe: wanting under a desirability characterization

In *Intention* (1957), Anscombe argues that wanting is not brute: if someone
wants something, they can always intelligibly be asked what for, or under
what aspect it strikes them as desirable, and a competent answer supplies a
**desirability characterization** — a description that renders the wanting
intelligible by connecting the wanted thing to *some* good-making feature
(pleasant, useful, needed, owed, kind, and so on). You cannot simply want
"gaining ten pounds" full stop; you want it *as* muscle mass, or *as* a
buffer against illness, or *as* a step toward some further good, and the
aspect chosen is exactly what supplies the normative direction. Wanting
something under no aspect at all is not a degenerate limit case of wanting —
Anscombe treats it as not really wanting in the practical-reason-relevant
sense; it would be unintelligible as action-explaining desire.

This is a direct structural diagnosis of "better for what?": Anscombe's
account says the question is not optional or a request for extra context —
it is *built into* what it is to want or prefer something at all. A bare
preference relation with no aspect attached (Better(Gain, Loss) with no
respect specified) is, on Anscombe's view, not a degraded or under-specified
version of a preference; it is not yet a preference in the sense that could
guide action, because nothing has been said about *what makes* gain
desirable here rather than there.

*Bearing:* this doesn't just say "add a facet/aspect" — it says an
unaspected preference was never coherent to state as a fact in the first
place, which reframes the four failures not as facets discovered late but as
the aspect being absent from day one.

## 4. Wiggins and McDowell on uncodifiability

**David Wiggins**, "Deliberation and Practical Reason" (*Proceedings of the
Aristotelian Society* 76, 1976; reprinted in *Needs, Values, Truth*, 1998),
reconstructs Aristotle's account of deliberation as reasoning toward a
particular action from a general conception of the good life, where the
particular situation is not merely an instance to be subsumed under a
general premise but something that can *itself* reshape what the agent takes
the relevant end to be. (Note on sourcing: I could not get past the
paywalled full text to verify Wiggins's exact wording on uncodifiability in
this pass — the characterization above is the standard secondary
characterization of the essay's role in the literature, not a verified direct
quotation. Flagging this as unverified/lower confidence.)

**John McDowell**, "Virtue and Reason" (1979) and related papers, states the
uncodifiability thesis sharply: the virtuous person's grasp of what a
situation requires is not "codifiable" — it cannot be written down as a set
of general rules that a person lacking the relevant sensitivity could pick up
and follow to reach the same verdicts. McDowell combines the Socratic
"virtue is knowledge" thesis with an Aristotelian "reliable sensitivity" to
the particular requirements of virtue, and treats this sensitivity as a
form of *perception* whose content resists full propositional/rule-like
articulation (McDowell later qualified how non-propositional this content
is, in response to Dreyfus's criticism, but the core uncodifiability claim
survives that qualification). The force of the claim is precisely that this
is not a *practical* limitation (rules would be too long or complicated to
write down) but a *principled* one: any codification a non-virtuous person
could mechanically apply would necessarily leave out exactly the
sensitivity that makes the application correct in novel or borderline cases.

*Bearing:* McDowell's version sharpens why "give the resolver more facets"
can't be a general fix — on his view the thing doing the work in a correct
judgment is not extra propositional content at all, but a perceptual
sensitivity that a facet-matching mechanism, however rich, is not the right
kind of thing to encode.

## 5. Nussbaum: fragility of goodness and the "science of measurement"

Martha Nussbaum, *The Fragility of Goodness: Luck and Ethics in Greek
Tragedy and Philosophy* (1986), argues that a recurring move in the Greek
tradition — most explicit in the Protagoras's proposed "art of measurement"
(*metrētikē technē*) — tries to dissolve the appearance of conflicting,
irreducibly plural values by supposing all value is really commensurable
along one dimension (pleasure, in the Protagoras), so that apparent
conflicts are merely miscalculations correctable by better measurement. If
there is only one good, quantifiable good, there cannot be genuine value
conflict — only error in comparing magnitudes of the same thing. Nussbaum
reads Plato (especially middle-period Plato) as extending this
"measurement" picture as an ideal of a fully rational, self-sufficient
ethical life immune to luck, in contrast to the tragedians' picture of
plural, sometimes incommensurable goods whose conflicts are not calculation
errors.

Her positive argument is that this reductive move is a *distortion* of
value, not a solution: goods like friendship, civic engagement, honor, and
erotic love are plural in a way that resists translation into a single
metric, and cases where they conflict (Agamemnon at Aulis is her paradigm
tragic case) are not failures of measurement but genuine, sometimes
tragic, incommensurabilities — situations where doing right by one value
constitutively requires wronging another, with no remainder-free resolution.
Nussbaum is explicit that she reads this as a live interpretive claim about
what the "measuring art" passage in the Protagoras is doing, and that her
reading (that Socrates's proposal there should be read with real
ambivalence/irony rather than as Plato's endorsed solution) is contested by
other Plato scholars — she is aware this is not the only defensible reading
of that dialogue.

*Bearing:* Nussbaum's target is precisely the assumption embedded in
`Better(Gain(), Loss())` as originally attempted — that there is some
single underlying quantity being measured — and her argument is that the
single-quantity picture is not merely hard to compute but a mischaracterization of what value is.

## 6. Hard choices as a philosophical topic

The clearest contemporary treatment is **Ruth Chang**'s work on
incommensurability and incomparability (see the SEP entry "Incommensurable
Values"). Two options are *incommensurable* if neither is better than the
other and they are not equally good either; they are *incomparable* if no
positive value relation holds between them at all (a narrower, stronger
claim once more than three value-relations are admitted). The classical
picture assumes a **trichotomy**: for any two comparable items, exactly one
of better/worse/equally-good holds. Chang argues this is incomplete and
proposes a fourth relation, **parity**: two items can be "on a par" —
genuinely comparable, in the same normative ballpark, without being equal
and without either being better. Her example: neither Mozart nor
Michelangelo is the more creative, but it would misdescribe the comparison
to call their creativity *equal* either — they are on a par along a
respect (creativity) that doesn't force a ranking or an equality.

The **small-improvement argument** is the technical engine behind this: take
A and B that are (apparently) equally good; improve A slightly to get A+.
Normally an improvement to one option makes it better than something it was
previously tied with. But in genuinely hard cases, A+ is still not better
than B, nor worse, nor equal — a result the trichotomy thesis cannot
accommodate without contradiction, which motivates positing a distinct
fourth relation rather than treating the case as a measurement failure.

On **whether hardness indicates ignorance or a genuine feature of value**,
the SEP entry frames two live positions: the **vagueness view** holds that
exactly one of better/worse/equal genuinely obtains in every case, and
hardness is semantic indeterminacy — we cannot tell *which* relation holds,
because "at least as good as" has borderline cases, much like "tall" does.
On this view hard choices are hard because of a gap in our
concepts/language, not because reality itself declines to settle the
question. The **parity view** (Chang's own) holds the opposite: parity is a
real, positive, sui generis value relation that actually obtains — hardness
is not a symptom of not-yet-resolved vagueness but a correct perception that
the values in play stand in a relation that isn't better, worse, or equal,
full stop. This is explicitly not resolved in the literature — the two
views make different commitments about whether more information or finer
concepts could in principle collapse a hard choice into an easy one, and
that disagreement is the recognized frontier of the topic, not a settled
matter.

*Bearing:* the vagueness-vs-parity split is the sharpest available framing
of whether "the resolver needs more facets" (vagueness diagnosis, fixable in
principle by more information) or "some conflicts are open ambiguities by
nature" (parity diagnosis, not fixable by more information) is the right way
to read Napkin's own choice to surface incomparable-but-equally-specific
cases as ambiguity rather than force a resolution.
