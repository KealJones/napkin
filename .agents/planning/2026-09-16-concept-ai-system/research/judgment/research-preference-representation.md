# Preference representation and defeasible reasoning — research notes

Scope: formal content, computational cost, and required inputs for six bodies of work relevant to representing conditional, state-dependent preference. No implementation proposal — research plus a one-line fit note per item.

## 1. CP-nets and ceteris paribus preference

**Formalism** (Boutilier, Brafman, Domshlak, Hoos, Poole, "CP-nets: A Tool for Representing and Reasoning with Conditional Ceteris Paribus Preference Statements", JAIR 2004 — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1107.0023, https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~cebly/Papers/CPnets.pdf). A CP-net is a directed graph over variables X1..Xn. Each variable has a domain, and for each variable a Conditional Preference Table (CPT) gives, for every assignment to its **parents** (the variables it depends on), a total or partial order over its own values, holding "all else equal" (ceteris paribus). Example: `Wine: Fish → White ≻ Red` means, holding every other feature fixed, white is preferred to red when the meal is fish. The CPT is the only place preference strength/order lives; the graph structure encodes conditional dependency between preference statements, not causal dependency between the variables themselves.

**Dominance queries.** The semantics of a CP-net is a preference order over complete outcomes, induced by taking the transitive closure of "worsening/improving flips" licensed by the CPTs. A dominance query asks whether outcome *o1* is preferred to *o2*; answering it means searching for a chain of ceteris-paribus flips connecting them. This is the operation that matters for the "which of two things is better" question.

**Complexity results (verified via arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3453, "The Computational Complexity of Dominance and Consistency in CP-Nets", Goldsmith, Lang, Truszczynski, Wilson):**
- Dominance testing in **general (possibly cyclic) CP-nets: PSPACE-complete**.
- Dominance testing in **acyclic CP-nets: NP-complete** (better than general case but still intractable in the worst case).
- Dominance testing in **tree-structured acyclic CP-nets: polynomial time**.
- Consistency checking (does a CP-net admit any total order at all — no preference cycles) follows the same stratification: hard in general, easier as the dependency graph is restricted.

This is a genuine finding worth sitting with: even the *easy* structural case (acyclic, non-tree) is NP-complete for a single pairwise "is A better than B" query. A general knowledge graph with many interacting preference statements would land in the cyclic/general case (PSPACE) unless the implementer deliberately restricts dependency structure to trees or bounded-width graphs.

**Input required that a knowledge graph wouldn't naturally have:** an explicit, per-variable, per-parent-assignment ordered CPT. This is elicited preference structure, not asserted fact — someone (or some default) has to author "given fish, white > red" as a first-class object separate from any relation like `Better(White, Red)`. It also assumes discrete, enumerable variable domains with a fixed parent set — awkward for arbitrary Concepts with open-ended relations.

**TCP-nets and successors.** TCP-nets (Brafman & Domshlak, "Introducing Variable Importance Tradeoffs into CP-nets" — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.0558) add a second edge type: relative-importance statements between *variables themselves* ("if A=a, optimizing B is more important than optimizing C"), letting one preference override another when they conflict, not just order values within one variable. This is the first mechanism in the CP-net family that resembles "which axis wins," rather than "what order does this axis have." Complexity is at least as hard as CP-nets (dominance testing remains PSPACE-hard in general; TCP-net-specific tractability results exist only for constrained subclasses — the CTCP-net/constrained variant integrates CSPs for optimization rather than reducing dominance complexity, per https://www.researchgate.net/publication/278965174). Later variants: probabilistic CP-nets/TCP-nets (weighted/uncertain preference statements) and mCP-nets (multi-agent, one CP-net per agent plus an aggregation rule — voting-style complexity results are separately studied and generally at least as hard, per ScienceDirect S0004370221001879).

**Fit note:** CP-nets formalize exactly the shape of the problem (conditional, ceteris-paribus preference that composes with context) but the "which variable's preference wins" question — the actual open problem here — is punted to TCP-nets' importance edges, which are themselves separately authored facts, not derived from anything the goal/state relationship would give you for free. And dominance testing is worst-case intractable outside restricted graph shapes, which matters if preference composition is expected to run per-query rather than be precompiled.

## 2. Defeasible / non-monotonic reasoning and specificity

**Default logic (Reiter, 1980).** A default theory is a pair (D, W): W is a set of classical first-order facts, D is a set of defaults of the form `A(x) : B(x) / C(x)` — "if prerequisite A holds, and justification B is *consistent* to assume, conclude C." An **extension** is a fixed point: a maximal set of consequences closed under classical inference and default application, such that no default's justification is contradicted within the extension itself. Multiple extensions can exist and are not automatically ranked — default logic alone has no built-in preference between conflicting defaults. (https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~mjs/teaching/KnowledgeRep491/DefaultLogic_491-2x1.pdf, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Default_logic)

**Specificity-based defeat.** This is the mechanism actually used to resolve conflicting defaults like Tweety: `Bird(x) : Flies(x)/Flies(x)` vs `Penguin(x) : ¬Flies(x)/¬Flies(x)`, given `Penguin(x) ⊃ Bird(x)`. The **specificity principle**: a defeasible rule with a more specific antecedent overrides a conflicting one with a less specific antecedent, where "more specific" is standardly defined via the strict-subset/strict-implication relation between antecedent classes (Penguin ⊂ Bird, not vice versa). Formally this is usually layered *on top of* base default logic or default/inheritance-net formalisms, because plain Reiter default logic has no notion of one default being "more specific" than another — it's a separate priority relation supplied externally (see the SEP entry https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-nonmonotonic/ and the specificity-principle sources above).

Two documented forms, and this is where it gets genuinely ambiguous:
- **Strong specificity**: defined via strict implication between antecedents (A ⇒ B, i.e., the more specific class is a logical subclass).
- **Weak specificity**: defined via a *defeasible* link between antecedents (A → B, i.e., "A's are normally B's," itself just another default) — this is circular/self-referential in a way strong specificity is not, and different papers resolve the circularity differently.

Specificity also **only produces a total order when the more-specific relation is itself a total (or at least consistent, acyclic) order on the conflicting rules**. When two defaults conflict on *different, incomparable* dimensions (e.g., "Quakers are pacifists" vs "Republicans aren't pacifists" for a Quaker Republican — the classic Nixon Diamond), specificity gives no verdict at all; standard default logic produces two extensions and most specificity-based systems (e.g., Horty's or Reiter/Criscuolo prioritized default logic) either report ambiguity or additional priority information must be supplied by hand.

**Circumscription (McCarthy).** Formalizes "assume as few abnormal individuals as possible" by minimizing an `Ab` predicate in second-order logic, model-theoretically rather than proof-theoretically. Prioritized circumscription lets some predicates be minimized before others, giving a specificity-like effect, but the priority order is again externally supplied, not derived.

**Answer Set Programming (ASP) with preferences.** ASP encodes defaults as rules with negation-as-failure and gets multiple "answer sets" (~extensions) when defaults conflict; a substantial literature (Brewka & Eiter "preferred answer sets," Delgrande/Schaub/Tompits, Sakama & Inoue, ordered logic programs) layers a priority/specificity order over *rules* to select preferred answer sets among the otherwise-incomparable ones (https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0301023, https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0003028, https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1017/S1471068404002315). Computationally this sits at the second level of the polynomial hierarchy for expressive variants (deciding whether an answer set is *preferred* is typically Σ²p or Θ²p-complete for disjunctive/prioritized extensions), i.e., strictly harder than plain ASP (already NP/co-NP for normal programs). The mechanical shape — rules ordered by a priority/specificity relation, conflicting conclusions resolved by that order, unordered/incomparable conflicts surfaced rather than resolved — is the closest existing formal analogue to "facet-specificity wins, equally-specific-on-different-facets is ambiguity."

**Fit note:** specificity-by-subset-count is exactly the CP-net/facet-subset selection mechanic already in the target system, which is reassuring — it means the system already has a working instance of the general mechanism the literature treats as an unsolved formal primitive (the strong/weak specificity distinction is essentially asking "is the subset relation between facet sets doing real epistemic work or just accidental structure," and for closed/enumerated facets that ambiguity mostly disappears). The genuine transferable warning: incomparable-specificity conflicts are a known, load-bearing, unresolved case in every one of these formalisms, not a bug to engineer away — the "surface the ambiguity" choice already made is the standard academic answer (Nixon Diamond), not a workaround.

## 3. Preference logics: von Wright, ceteris paribus semantics, proposition vs. outcome

**Von Wright's Logic of Preference (1963).** The founding formal preference logic. Preferences are between *propositions/states of affairs* (p ≻ q), and crucially are **unconditional but holistic**: "p is preferred to q" is cashed out as "any complete state of the world containing p-and-not-q is preferred to any otherwise-identical complete state containing q-and-not-p" — i.e., von Wright's ceteris paribus is baked into the semantics as a comparison over *total possible worlds* differing only in p/q, not a surface syntactic operator (https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2025/entries/logic-preference/, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-009-9530-z).

**Proposition-preference vs. outcome-preference — the actual distinction that matters here.** Preferring a *proposition* (p ≻ q) is a claim about the truth-value-level comparison and is what most preference logics (including von Wright's) formalize; preferring an *outcome* is a claim about a comparison between fully specified world-states or acts, which is what decision theory and CP-nets actually need for practical recommendation. The gap between the two is exactly where "ceteris paribus" does its work: a proposition-preference has to be turned into an outcome-preference by holding everything *else* about the two worlds fixed, and how you decide what counts as "else" is not fixed by the logic — it's a modeling choice (van Benthem, van Otterloo, Roy, "A Modal Logic for Ceteris Paribus Preferences" formalizes this explicitly by parametrizing ceteris paribus by a *set of relevant propositions* Γ that must be held equal: https://www.pgrim.org/philosophersannual/29articles/vanbenthemeverything.pdf, Journal of Philosophical Logic version http://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10992-008-9085-3.pdf). Changing Γ changes what the preference operator even means, and there is no logic-internal way to pick Γ correctly for a given decision.

**Fit note:** this literature makes precise something the problem statement is already implicitly wrestling with — "better" is not a 2-place relation on propositions at all without first fixing a Γ (what's held constant) and a comparison base (what "otherwise identical" means for a specific decision). Von Wright-style logics give you the *syntax* for conditional preference statements but explicitly leave "what varies, what's held fixed" to be supplied externally — they formalize the shape of the problem without solving the reference-point piece.

## 4. Description logics with defaults

DLs are decidable fragments of FOL by design; adding classical nonmonotonic default logic or autoepistemic logic on top routinely **destroys decidability**, because default/autoepistemic constructs interact with DL's restricted quantification in ways that reintroduce full FOL expressiveness (source: arXiv 1401.3476/DL circumscription complexity papers, and the DL^N overview https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.04978). Two documented mitigation paths, both with real costs:

- **Circumscription over DLs** (Bonatti, Lutz, Wolter, "Description Logics with Circumscription"): minimizing an abnormality predicate stays decidable *only* under restriction — specifically, only concept names may be minimized/circumscribed while role names must be fixed (not varying); if role names are allowed to vary during minimization, reasoning becomes **undecidable already in basic ALC**. Under the safe restriction, complexity for expressive DLs reaches **NEXPTIME^NP-complete** — a large jump above the already-not-cheap complexity of the base DL (source verified via https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3476, ACM DL abstract).
- **Rational closure / typicality-based defeasible DLs** (Giordano, Gliozzi, Olivetti, and others; DL^N): a lighter-weight approach adding "typical/normal" individuals and closing defeasible subsumption under a KLM-style rational-closure construction, which stays closer to the base DL's complexity class but gives up some inferential strength (specificity-style overriding is limited to what rational closure entails, not full prioritized default logic).

**Input required that a KG wouldn't naturally have:** an explicit minimize/vary/fix partition over predicates (circumscription) or a typicality ranking over individuals/concepts (rational closure) — again, externally authored structure, and in both cases the DL research explicitly flags that this is *why* defaults are hard to bolt onto DLs soundly: DL decidability is bought by restricting quantification, and virtually every natural way of adding "unless" reopens that restriction.

**Fit note:** direct cautionary tale for any Concept-graph-as-DL-like-structure design: if Napkin's realizations/relations ever get formalized as a description logic for soundness/decidability guarantees, adding facet-based defeasibility on top is exactly the move that this literature shows tends to blow up complexity or decidability unless very carefully restricted.

## 5. Argumentation frameworks: Dung, and value-based (Bench-Capon)

**Dung's abstract argumentation framework (1995).** AF = ⟨Args, →⟩: a set of arguments and an attack relation, with no internal structure to arguments at all. Semantics (grounded, preferred, stable, complete) define which *sets* of jointly-acceptable arguments ("extensions") survive: an argument is acceptable relative to a set S if S defends it (attacks every attacker of it). Grounded semantics gives the unique minimal, skeptical extension; preferred/stable semantics can be non-unique, mirroring the multiple-extensions phenomenon in default logic (they are formally closely related — default logic and Dung AFs are known to be instantiable from each other). (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-75526-5_4 and related.)

**Value-based argumentation frameworks, VAF (Bench-Capon, 2002/2003).** Extends Dung AFs by attaching each argument to a **value** it promotes, and adding an **audience**: a (partial or total) ordering of values reflecting what that audience/agent cares about. An attack only *succeeds* (defeats) if the attacked argument's value is not preferred, by that audience, to the attacker's value — otherwise the attack fails and both arguments can stand. Consequently, **the same AF with the same arguments and the same attack relation yields different accepted conclusions for different audiences**, and disagreement between two audiences need not mean either is "wrong" — Bench-Capon's explicit motivation is practical/moral reasoning where the point of argument is persuasion given differing values, not proof of a single correct answer. (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221535745_Value-based_argumentation_frameworks, https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3135746/, audiences paper https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000437020600110X)

**Complexity:** VAF acceptability inherits Dung AF complexity as a baseline (credulous acceptance under preferred semantics is NP-complete; skeptical acceptance under preferred semantics is Π²p-complete for general AFs), and computing acceptability *per audience* is essentially one Dung-AF-acceptability computation per value-ordering, so complexity is dominated by the underlying AF semantics rather than by the value layer itself, but the value-ordering has to already be given per audience — VAFs don't derive it.

**Input required that a KG wouldn't naturally have:** (a) a decomposition of each preference-relevant fact into an *argument* with an attack relation to competing arguments, which is heavier machinery than a bare relational fact, and (b) an explicit value-ordering per audience/agent, supplied from outside the graph.

**Fit note:** this is the strongest structural match to "the weights belong to the person, not the graph." VAF cleanly separates (1) the graph-level structure of which considerations conflict with which (attack relation — this can plausibly live in the Concept graph as shared, objective structure) from (2) the person-level ranking of values that decides which conflicts resolve which way (audience ordering — this is explicitly *not* part of the AF, it's supplied per-agent). That separation is precisely the missing third term in the Bodyweight/Dollar problem: the graph encodes that Gain and Loss conflict and what goals they each serve, and a per-agent (or per-query) value-ordering — supplied at evaluation time, not stored as a graph fact — decides the winner. The cost is architectural: it requires modeling preference conflicts as first-class attack relations between arguments/relations rather than as plain asserted facts, which is a heavier unit than Napkin's current three-part Concept.

## 6. Preferences conditional on a reference point / current state

This is the thinnest formal literature of the six, and it lives in economics/psychology, not KR. **Prospect theory** (Kahneman & Tversky) and the reference-dependence literature formalize value as `v(outcome − reference point)`, with the reference point itself being a free parameter of the model — typically the status quo, an expectation, a goal, or a lagged consumption level — that must be independently specified or *revealed* from observed choices (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_dependence, revealed-reference-point paper https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00199-017-1096-2, reference-point theory of risk preference https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17456916231190393). Two structural facts worth carrying over:

- The value function is typically **asymmetric around the reference point** (losses loom larger than equivalent gains) and its **curvature flips sign** on either side (risk-averse for gains, risk-seeking for losses) — this is precisely the shape of "Loss is bad above a healthy weight, good below it," except prospect theory's reference point is about gains/losses in the same variable, not a threshold on the variable's own value, so the analogy needs adjusting rather than lifting wholesale.
- Formal decision theory generally treats the reference point as **exogenous input to the model, not something the theory derives** — there is no widely accepted formal account of *how* a reference point is computed from context that could be mechanically ported into a KR system; "status quo," "goal," and "expectation" are three different, non-unified proposals in the literature, and empirical work (Mellers & Yin, reference-point theory of individual differences) treats which one applies as an individual-difference/measurement question, not a derivable fact.

**Input required that a KG wouldn't naturally have:** an explicit, agent- and moment-specific reference point (or a stated rule for deriving one — status quo vs. goal vs. expectation), which is exactly the missing ingredient the Bodyweight example needs and which no other formalism in sections 1-5 supplies either — CP-nets, VAFs, and default logic all take the *conflict structure* as given but none of them models "preference flips sign depending on where you currently are relative to a target."

**Fit note:** no ready-made formal machinery to import here — this is the one area where the state-of-the-art is "yes, this matters, here's the phenomenon, the reference point is still an unexplained free parameter of the model" rather than a mechanism to borrow. Whatever primitive Napkin adds for this will likely have no direct precedent to lean on; it's the genuinely novel piece of the six.

## Cross-cutting synthesis

Every formalism surveyed cleanly separates two things: (1) *structure* — what conflicts with what, what depends on what, which is safely graph/fact-like — and (2) *a resolution ordering supplied from outside the structure* — CPT orderings, specificity/priority relations, circumscription's minimize/fix partition, audience value-orderings, or reference points. None of the six derive (2) from (1); all of them require it as separate input, confirming that "preference cannot be a plain relation in the graph" is not a design failure to route around but a result that recurs across KR, philosophy, and decision theory literatures independently. The two most actionable structural ideas for a facet/specificity-based system are: CP-net-style ceteris-paribus conditioning (preference statements scoped by a context, matched by specificity — this is close to already built) and VAF's attack/value separation (conflicts are graph structure, but the ordering over what resolves the conflict is supplied per-agent/per-query, never stored as a fact).
