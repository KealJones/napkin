# Prior systems on context-dependent and conflicting knowledge — what was tried, what happened

## 1. Cyc microtheories

A microtheory (Mt) is a bundled set of assertions sharing common assumptions — a domain (`GeometryGMt`), a time slice, a level of detail, a believer, or a point of view. Every CycL assertion is technically indexed to a microtheory context; there is no global, context-free truth. Microtheories form a partial order under `genlMt` ("more general microtheory"): an assertion in a general Mt is visible (inherited) from any of its specializations unless the specialization overrides it. Querying Cyc always means querying *relative to* a chosen microtheory (or lifting the query up the `genlMt` chain). This is exactly the "unordered facets, more specific wins" idea, generalized: Cyc's version is a hierarchy (partial order) rather than an arbitrary facet-set, so specificity is defined by graph position, not by counting matched facets.

**Handling of the same fact true in one Mt, false in another:** Cyc does not try to merge the two into one consistent global theory. `Better(Gain, Loss)` for `FinanceMt` and its negation for `BodyWeightMt` can coexist because they're never compared directly — each query names (or is scoped to) a specific Mt, and Cyc's inference engine treats different Mts as separate, only locally-consistent worlds. The trick is deliberately *not* solving global consistency: two mutually contradictory assertions never collide because no query ever spans both microtheories at once unless a human explicitly builds a "lifting" bridge (see `EnglishMt` style translation axioms and works like "lifting rules" mentioned in the Cyc literature) that translates one Mt's vocabulary/assumptions into a more general one, adding qualifying clauses as it goes (e.g., "ownership is a precondition for consumption" only lifts to a general context once you add "law-abiding, calm, responsible parties in a capitalist economy" as a rider). Source: AAAI AI Magazine Cyc overview (ojs.aaai.org/aimagazine, Lenat & Guha era description); CycL Wikipedia page; GM-RKB Cyc Ontology page.

**Honest retrospective.** This is the load-bearing part, and the record is bad:

- After roughly 40 years, ~$200M, ~2000 person-years, and on the order of tens of millions of hand-entered assertions, Cyc did not reach the "common sense maturity" its founders predicted. Lenat and Guha wrote in 1989 that "by 1999 no one would even think about having a computer that doesn't have Cyc running on it" — a prediction the project fell far short of. (mjtsai.com/blog/2025/04/14/obituary-for-cyc; outsiderart.substack.com/p/cyc-historys-forgotten-ai-project)
- Pedro Domingos (machine-learning researcher, author of *The Master Algorithm*) has called Cyc "a catastrophic failure," citing the unending, combinatorially expanding hand-authoring burden and Cyc's inability to learn or extend itself. (same source thread, widely cited)
- Ernest Davis (NYU), a serious symbolic-AI researcher sympathetic to the project's goals, wrote a dedicated evaluation ("Evaluating CYC: Preliminary Notes," cs.nyu.edu/~davise/papers/CYCEval.pdf) specifically because Cyc's actual behavior was opaque to outsiders — most claims about what Cyc could do were unverifiable by anyone outside Cycorp, itself a symptom of the problem (a closed, proprietary KB that the field could not independently evaluate). I was not able to extract this PDF's full text programmatically, so treat this citation as pointing to a real, on-topic source rather than a verified quote.
- Gary Marcus (who co-authored a final paper with Lenat before Lenat's 2023 death, arXiv 2308.04445 "Getting from Generative AI to Trustworthy AI: What LLMs might learn from Cyc") gives the more moderate insider-adjacent verdict: Cyc was "neither a success nor a failure... a ground-breaking, clarion experiment that never fully gelled," and the corrective the Lenat/Marcus paper argues for is hybridizing LLMs with Cyc-style explicit, inspectable structure — implicitly conceding LLMs solved the coverage/breadth problem that hand-built microtheories never did. (garymarcus.substack.com/p/doug-lenat-1950-2023)
- Common specific complaints collected across sources: the logic/language-like representation format was hard to scale and hard to author correctly at large volumes; the project rebuilt/refactored large chunks of its own ontology repeatedly rather than incrementally maintaining one; it optimized for answering closed questions rather than acting in the world; and it stayed a private, proprietary KB rather than an open one the community could pressure-test — which undercuts exactly the kind of "is this microtheory design actually working" verification you'd want. (outsiderart.substack.com/p/cyc-historys-forgotten-ai-project; mjtsai.com obituary roundup)

**Verdict on microtheories specifically:** the mechanism is architecturally sound and is the most direct working precedent for "unordered facet context + specificity ordering" — it demonstrably let Cyc hold `Better(Gain,Loss)` and its negation without contradiction. What failed was not the context mechanism per se, it was everything around it: authoring cost scaled worse than linearly as microtheories multiplied (someone has to write the lifting/bridging rules between every pair of Mts that need to interact, and that combinatorics is exactly the "how many hand-authored bridges do we need" problem), verification was impossible from outside, and the system never developed a way to learn new microtheories or bridge rules on its own. A knowledge system that needs a human to write a lifting axiom every time two contexts need to talk to each other does not scale past a few hundred contexts.

**Fit note:** Cnocept's facet-set-by-subset-match is actually a cleaner design than Cyc's `genlMt` partial order for *this* specific problem (context selection is now a computed property of the query's facets, not something curated by hand), but Cnocept should expect the same authoring-scaling risk Cyc hit if disjoint facet-vocabularies need cross-translation — worth deciding early whether Cnocept ever needs an analogue of "lifting" (translating a fact from one facet-context to a broader one) or deliberately refuses to, the way Cyc's failure suggests hand-written lifting doesn't scale.

## 2. McCarthy's formal theory of context: `ist(c, p)`

John McCarthy's "Notes on Formalizing Context" (IJCAI 1993; expanded with Sasa Buvac, 1998) proposed a formal semantics where propositions are never simply true or false, only true-in-a-context: `ist(c, p)` reads "proposition p is true in context c." Contexts are first-class objects of the logic (not comments or annotations) so you can quantify over them, compare them, and reason about entering/exiting a context.

The key move meant to solve exactly the problem Cnocept has (general laws that don't hold everywhere): **lifting axioms**. A fact proved in an "inner," more specific context can be "lifted" to an "outer," more general context by adding qualifying clauses — the classic example given is that block-world axioms proven true in a static "situation" context can be lifted to a more general context where situations change, provided you add the extra machinery needed to talk about change. Structurally this is the same move Cyc's microtheory lifting rules do — McCarthy's formalism is in fact the theoretical ancestor Cyc cites for its own contexts.

**What came of it, honestly:** McCarthy's theory was influential as *logic* — it spawned a real research subfield (Propositional Logic of Context / PLC, and a rival formalism, Local Models Semantics / Multi-Context Systems, from Giunchiglia and Ghidini) — but it never became a widely adopted engineering substrate. A directly relevant technical result: Ghidini and Giunchiglia (Springer LNCS, "On the Difference between Bridge Rules and Lifting Axioms") proved that lifting axioms (McCarthy's PLC mechanism) are strictly *less expressive* than "bridge rules" (the MCS mechanism) — there exist contextual reasoning patterns MCS can capture with bridge rules that PLC cannot capture with lifting axioms alone. In other words, even within the formal-logic community, McCarthy's specific proposed mechanism for connecting contexts was shown to be theoretically inadequate for some cases, and a competing formalism displaced it in expressiveness (though not in fame — McCarthy's `ist(c,p)` remains the more commonly cited framing). Neither formalism, to my knowledge, became a deployed large-scale KB; Cyc borrowed McCarthy's vocabulary and framing but built its own more pragmatic, non-fully-formalized version (genlMt) rather than the strict PLC.

**Fit note:** the theoretical result (bridge rules > lifting axioms in expressive power) is a useful cautionary data point if Cnocept's facet-based context selection is ever formalized as pure "lift the assertion to a broader context" — the literature says a pure lifting approach provably can't express everything a cross-context bridging rule can.

## 3. SUMO and DOLCE: evaluative predicates

Neither upper ontology treats evaluation ("better," "good") as a first-class primitive relation, and neither appears to have a documented mechanism for reference-dependent gradability. What they do have that's adjacent:

- **DOLCE** models scalar properties via **qualities located in quality spaces/regions**: e.g., "the weight of John" is an individual *quality* that has a *location* (a value) inside a *quality space* (a region structure, e.g. a weight scale). This is a real, working representation for "heavy/light" as measurements, but it is a measurement model, not an evaluation model — DOLCE doesn't natively encode "better" or "good" as a relation between two things, and doesn't have a documented treatment of comparison-class-relative predicates (a DOLCE weight-quality is an absolute point on a scale; "heavy for a mouse" requires an external convention DOLCE doesn't specify). Source: LOA (loa.istc.cnr.it) DOLCE foundational papers; arXiv 2308.01597 "DOLCE: A Descriptive Ontology..."
- **SUMO** is a much larger, more domain-covering ontology built with first-order logic and (unlike DOLCE) explicit ties to WordNet senses, but the available documentation and survey papers found in this research pass did not surface an evaluative-predicate treatment either — SUMO has predicates for physical and mereological structure, not a documented axiomatization of gradable/evaluative comparison. I could not find a credible source describing how (or whether) SUMO handles "X is better than Y" as a formal predicate; treat "SUMO does not represent evaluative predicates" as an inference from absence of documentation, not a verified negative.

**Fit note:** both projects solved "how do you represent a scalar quantity" (DOLCE's quality/region model is genuinely reusable framing) but neither solved "how do you represent an evaluative comparison that flips sign depending on context" — that gap is real and Cnocept's problem sits squarely in it. This is a case of "nobody built this part," not "somebody built this part and it failed."

## 4. ConceptNet

ConceptNet 5 has ~34 relation types (`/r/` namespace): structural (IsA, PartOf, HasA), functional (UsedFor, CapableOf, CreatedBy), spatial (AtLocation, LocatedNear), event (Causes, HasSubevent, HasPrerequisite), descriptive (HasProperty, DefinedAs, SymbolOf), and lexical (Synonym, Antonym, DistinctFrom, FormOf). There is **no dedicated evaluative relation** — no `BetterThan`, no `IsGood`. The closest tools are `HasProperty` (e.g., ice → cold) and `Antonym` (hot ↔ cold), which encode a property or its opposition but not a ranked/scalar comparison. Source: github.com/commonsense/conceptnet5/wiki/Relations.

**Contradiction handling:** ConceptNet has **no context mechanism**. Each assertion carries a scalar confidence *weight* derived from how many independent sources/contributors asserted it (crowd-sourced, e.g. via the Open Mind Common Sense project and games with a purpose), but this is a popularity/agreement score, not a truth-conditional context tag — two flatly contradictory assertions about the same pair of concepts simply both sit in the graph with their own weights, and downstream consumers must decide how to reconcile them (typically: prefer the higher-weight edge, or return both and let the application context-filter). There is a deprecated `Not*` relation family (NotDesires, NotUsedFor, NotCapableOf, NotHasProperty) meant to capture explicit negative assertions from certain crowd-sourced batches, but the wiki flags these as legacy/deprecated with no documented resolution strategy against their positive counterparts.

**Authors' own stated limitations:** ConceptNet's own documentation and evaluation papers acknowledge it is noisy, informal, subjective, and often only true "under some circumstances and to some extent" — one evaluation found roughly 15.5% of assertions judged "false or vague" by human raters. This is a direct, citable admission that ConceptNet never solved (or really attempted) the context-dependent-truth problem; it pushed the problem downstream by attaching a confidence number instead of a context. Source: github.com/commonsense/conceptnet5/wiki/Graph-structure; ACL/ACM papers on ConceptNet path quality (dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3308558.3313486).

**Fit note:** ConceptNet is the clearest "didn't even try" case among the systems surveyed — useful as the low bar: a confidence-weighted, context-free graph. Cnocept's facet-context system is already a strict improvement over this baseline in kind, not just degree.

## 5. WordNet and FrameNet: gradable adjectives

**WordNet** organizes descriptive ("ascriptive") adjectives into **antonym clusters** ("dumbbells"): a head synset for each pole of a bipolar scale (e.g., *large* / *small*), with satellite synsets clustered around each pole by similarity (*large* pole: gigantic, huge, monstrous; *small* pole: tiny, pocketsize, smallish), and the two poles linked by direct antonymy like an axle connecting two wheels. This captures the *lexical* structure of a scale (what words share a scale and which end they're on) but is explicitly **not** a model of comparison-class-relative truth — WordNet doesn't encode that "large for a mouse" and "large for an elephant" pick out different absolute weights.

**The linguistics term you want** is Christopher Kennedy's distinction between **relative** and **absolute gradable adjectives** (Kennedy, "Vagueness and Grammar: The Semantics of Relative and Absolute Gradable Adjectives," *Linguistics & Philosophy* 2007, building on his 1999 dissertation). Relative gradable adjectives (*big, heavy, expensive, good*) have a **context-dependent standard of comparison** set by an implicit or explicit **comparison class** — "big" applied to a mouse invokes the comparison class of mice, so "a big mouse" and "a big elephant" pick out very different absolute sizes; hence the classic example, a big mouse is smaller than a small elephant. Absolute gradable adjectives (*full, straight, dry*) are anchored to a fixed scale endpoint and don't shift with comparison class the same way. Kennedy's account treats this via a "domain restriction" that supplies the comparison class from context, with truth conditions computed relative to that class-relative standard, only falling back on further context-dependence when the lexical scale structure itself doesn't resolve it ("interpretive economy"). This is a genuinely mature, formally worked-out linguistic theory of exactly the phenomenon (`Better(Gain,Loss)` for money vs. bodyweight is a comparison-class problem in this framing) — but it is a semantic theory of natural language interpretation, not a knowledge-representation system; no major KB in this survey (Cyc, ConceptNet, SUMO, DOLCE) has adopted Kennedy-style comparison-class parameters as a formal device. Source: semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy (multiple Kennedy PDFs); Wikipedia "Opposite" (gradable antonyms).

**FrameNet** comes closer to a genuine model of the Cnocept problem than any KB surveyed. Its **Desirability** frame explicitly represents evaluative predicates ("good," "excellent," "bad") with core frame elements *Evaluee* and *Parameter* (the scalar property being evaluated), plus non-core elements *Circumstances*, *Comparison_set* (a set of same-type entities the evaluation is relative to), *Affected_party*, and *Degree*. This is essentially a hand-built, linguistically-motivated version of "evaluation is a relation with an implicit context argument," including an explicit slot for the comparison class and for whom the evaluation is relative to. It is a frame for annotating text (used in frame-semantic parsing / SRL), not a queryable world-knowledge base, so it doesn't store or reconcile actual conflicting evaluative facts — but its frame structure is a legitimate, tested schema for what the *arguments* of a context-sensitive evaluative predicate should be. Source: FrameNet Desirability frame (framenet.icsi.berkeley.edu, cited via ACL/arXiv secondary sources).

**Fit note:** Kennedy's comparison-class theory and FrameNet's Desirability frame are the two most conceptually on-point pieces of prior work in this whole survey for `Better(Gain,Loss)`-style problems — worth citing directly in the design doc as evidence the "evaluation needs an implicit comparison-class/context argument" analysis is linguistically well-established, not a novel claim Cnocept is making alone.

## 6. Modern knowledge-graph practice: named graphs, reification, RDF-star, Wikidata qualifiers/ranks

RDF's base data model (subject-predicate-object triples) has no room for context by default, which is why the ecosystem grew several bolt-on mechanisms:
- **Reification** (the original 1999 RDF mechanism): turn a triple into a resource with `rdf:subject/predicate/object` properties so you can attach metadata (source, time, confidence) to the *statement itself*. Widely regarded as verbose and rarely used in practice because it triples (no pun intended) the storage and query complexity for every contextualized fact.
- **Named graphs**: partition a triple store into multiple graphs, each identified by a URI, so a set of triples can be scoped to a source/context/time and queried per-graph or across graphs (SPARQL's `GRAPH` keyword). This is structurally close to Cyc's microtheories — a named graph is a bag of triples true "in" that graph — but named graphs are typically used for provenance/dataset separation, not for encoding logical context hierarchies with inheritance; there's no standard `genlMt`-style specialization order between named graphs.
- **RDF-star (RDF*) / SPARQL-star**: a newer W3C-track extension letting a triple itself be the subject or object of another triple (`<<:s :p :o>> :confidence 0.9`), giving statement-level annotation without reification's verbosity. This is the current best-practice successor to reification for "annotate this specific fact with metadata," but it is still annotation (weight/provenance/time), not a context-selection/specificity-ranking mechanism.
- **Wikidata's qualifiers and ranks** are the most mature, large-scale, actually-deployed answer here. Every statement (`claim`) can carry qualifiers — extra key-value pairs that constrain when/where/how the statement holds, e.g., population=66.6M qualified by "excluding Adélie Land," or a claim qualified by "point in time: 2005" and "determination method: estimation," or "applies to jurisdiction: X" for a spatial validity context. Wikidata's own documentation describes the validity context of a statement as spanning multiple dimensions — time, space, applicable work, taxon, etc. — matched via qualifiers. Separately, **ranks** (preferred / normal / deprecated) let editors mark which of several qualifier-differentiated statements should "win" by default for display/query purposes when multiple are simultaneously true (e.g., a country's current population is "preferred," historical populations are "normal"). Source: wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Qualifiers, Help:Statements, Help:Ranking; ResearchGate "Handling Wikidata Qualifiers in Reasoning."

Wikidata's model is qualifiers-as-facets plus a rank as a manual specificity/preference override, at genuine web-scale, with millions of editors. It is the closest thing in real production use to Cnocept's "context is a set of facets" idea — but note two honest limits: (1) qualifiers are attached per-statement, not composed into a reusable named context object the way Cnocept's context/facet-sets are meant to be reused across many realizations, so there's no Wikidata equivalent of "this whole context applies to many facts at once"; and (2) rank is a blunt, manually-curated override for *display/query default*, not a computed specificity ordering — Wikidata does not automatically infer that a qualifier set with more constraints should "win" over a less-constrained one the way subset-matching does. Both are useful contrasts: Wikidata solved "attach the conditions to a fact" at scale but did not solve "automatically pick the most specific applicable fact from competing qualifier-sets," which is closer to what Cnocept's facet-subset-matching realization dispatch is attempting.

## Summary table (informal)

| System | Context mechanism | Handles conflicting facts? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyc | Microtheories + genlMt inheritance + hand-written lifting | Yes, by scoping — never merges | Mechanism worked; authoring/verification/scaling around it did not. Widely called a failure or "never fully gelled." |
| McCarthy `ist(c,p)` | Formal logic, contexts as objects, lifting axioms | Theoretically, yes | Influential in logic; shown less expressive than rival (bridge rules); never deployed at scale. |
| SUMO / DOLCE | None documented for evaluative predicates; DOLCE has quality/region for scalars | No | Gap not filled by either; DOLCE's quality-space model reusable for measurement, not evaluation. |
| ConceptNet | None — flat graph + confidence weight | No, just weighted coexistence | Authors admit ~15% false/vague; context-dependence explicitly out of scope. |
| WordNet | Antonym clusters (lexical only) | No | Structure, not truth-conditional context; doesn't touch comparison-class shift. |
| Kennedy (linguistics) | Comparison-class-parameterized semantics | Yes, formally, for language | Mature theory, never adopted by a KB. |
| FrameNet Desirability | Frame with Comparison_set/Circumstances slots | Represents it for annotation | Good schema, not a live reasoning system. |
| RDF reification / named graphs / RDF-star | Statement-level or graph-level annotation | Partial | Mostly provenance/versioning tools, not specificity ranking. |
| Wikidata qualifiers + ranks | Per-statement qualifier facets + manual rank | Yes, at web scale | Most successful deployed precedent; manual rank, not computed specificity; no reusable shared-context object. |
